Tap into the curiosity that led you to grad school in the first place.

One challenge in higher ed is that the expected pathways are so rigidly defined that it’s hard to envision different possibilities, let alone pursue them.

In that context, how do you even know what you might want to do?

Starting from the question of what really makes you light up creates a completely different posture towards a career search. Discernment and discovery can be a really difficult part of the process, not least because they have a strong emotional charge to them. But like the questions I asked yesterday, the discernment process taps into our deep curiosity—which is often part of what brought us to graduate school in the first place, but something that can be hard to tap into when you’re scrambling to keep up.

There are tools and resources that can help. The MSU Graduate School website has a great Career Development section, and I have quite a few resources gathered at the link and QR code on the slide. Let me tell you more about a few of my favorites.

Taking stock

Take a few minutes to reflect and write:
  • What gives you energy in your work right now? What drains it?
  • What would you like more of in the year ahead — in work, and beyond it?
  • One small step you could take this month
Give a full five minutes of quiet writing time. Responses are for them — no one has to share. This loops back to the first step in "Where do I even start?": assessing what's working and what's not. Encourage them to keep the last answer small and concrete.

What the research tells us

1 Advisor quality is decisive.
2 Funding is systematically inadequate.
3 Peer communities are lifelines.
4 Early career guidance matters.
5 Curricular clarity supports success.
ACLS Doctoral Futures Initiative, 2025–26

Through this work, a few things have emerged as key elements for student success:

  1. Advisor quality. A supportive advisor is perhaps the most crucial element in student progress and well-being. However, advising practices vary significantly and are not often institutionally rewarded, leaving students in a precarious position without clear guidelines to navigate challenges. This is something I’ll be talking about in depth in the faculty workshop tomorrow.

  2. Material support. Adequate funding is essential to student progress, but the typical stipend falls short of a living wage. Students face food insecurity and impossible trade-offs. If you can't pay rent, care for your kid, or go to the doctor, there's no way you can do your best intellectual work.

  3. Non-competitive peer networks. Informal peer networks provide crucial support, especially when formal structures fail. Grad students regularly turn to their peers to understand the hidden curriculum, to form writing groups, and so much more. In many institutions right now, program size reductions are making this increasingly difficult.

  4. Career guidance. While grad career advising has improved in many institutions, for many students the discussion starts too late, toward the end of their studies. It should be integrated into the entire curriculum—not just a standalone career center, but in discussions within the department—from the very beginning of a student's program. Even before someone applies, they should have a sense of where alumni tend to be employed and what their own career prospects might be.

  5. Curricular clarity supports success. Students—and really, faculty—should know why each step of their program matters. What is the goal of the exam? What constitutes a successful dissertation? Alongside this clarity, flexibility can provide essential breathing room—either for non-traditional research approaches, or for the unexpected curveballs that are a part of life. Clear guidelines plus space for creative work and timing adjustments leads to stronger progress and more meaningful research.

These are not impossible changes! While some of these interventions—like funding—require significant resources, not all of them do. Some of the changes could be made right now with sufficient collective will.
