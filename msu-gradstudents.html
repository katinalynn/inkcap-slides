Tap into the curiosity that led you to grad school in the first place.

One challenge in higher ed is that the expected pathways are so rigidly defined that it’s hard to envision different possibilities, let alone pursue them.

In that context, how do you even know what you might want to do?

Starting from the question of what really makes you light up creates a completely different posture towards a career search. Discernment and discovery can be a really difficult part of the process, not least because they have a strong emotional charge to them. But like the questions I asked yesterday, the discernment process taps into our deep curiosity—which is often part of what brought us to graduate school in the first place, but something that can be hard to tap into when you’re scrambling to keep up.

There are tools and resources that can help. The MSU Graduate School website has a great Career Development section, and I have quite a few resources gathered at the link and QR code on the slide. Let me tell you more about a few of my favorites.

Principles for the path

  • Know what you need.
  • Guard your time.
  • Seek resources before you need them.
  • Name and own your skills.
  • Find your people.

I want to share a few guiding principles to keep in mind as you move through your path:

  • First, be mindful of what you need and of what brings you joy. Return to that earlier question of what thriving looks like. Don't lose sight of it, even when the system discourages you from asking.

  • Guard your time. You don't have to say yes to everything. Sometimes it's worth taking on new projects; sometimes it's not.

  • Seek out and use resources well before you need them. Lots of people have been working on these questions and there are many tools to support you, if you are open to them.

  • Name and really OWN your skills. In grad school, it can sometimes be hard to see what makes you special, because everyone around you is operating at such a high level as well. But the skills you gain here are incredibly useful. Teaching, writing, research design, community engagement — practice articulating these in multiple registers.

  • Finally, and maybe most important: Find your people. Connect with peers -- Create a writing group, connect with people outside the university, figure out who you can lean on. Identify mentors -- Who among the faculty will have your back when you need it? Maybe your advisor, maybe someone else. Always be building relationships—not only because networking is important professionally, but because these are the relationships that sustain us.

Taking stock

Take five minutes to write. Start where step one begins: what's working for you, and what's not?
  • What gives you energy in your work right now? What drains it?
  • What would you like more of in the year ahead — in work, and beyond it?
  • One small step you could take this month
Give a full five minutes of quiet writing time. Responses are for them — no one has to share. This loops back to the first step in "Where do I even start?": assessing what's working and what's not. Encourage them to keep the last answer small and concrete.

Let's talk

  • What do you wish you had known, or had access to, earlier in your program?
  • What kinds of support could you build with and for your peers?
  • What questions are you still carrying?
