What I'm drawing on

The talk I'm giving today draws on both my first book, Putting the Humanities PhD to Work, and my forthcoming book, titled Unexpected Flourishing: Growth from Decay in the Mycelial University. It's a book about the current state of higher education, and even though decay is right there in the title, it takes a stance not of despair, but of critical hope. That is the same stance I want to bring today.

I finished my own PhD sixteen years ago, in 2010. One thing that has remained consistent across all my work in that time is the desire to move from the question of individual actions to structural change.

There are several key anchor points that I return to again and again; these were central to that first book and remain important today:

  1. Talking about career diversity is not enough. I see too many universities tack this on top of structures that do not support or enable the kind of curiosity and exploration that is essential to opening up new avenues of possibility.

  2. Structures reveal and reinforce values. A university might say it supports access and equity, but if its structures are all grounded in prestige, its stated values will not carry much weight. Real values are often most apparent through institutional budgets.

  3. Public engagement supports students, the academy, AND society. Not all scholarship needs to be grounded in community engagement, but when it is, it's crucial to celebrate not only the knowledge but also the relationships as part of the scholarly achievement. We NEED this work in order to continue strengthening public advocacy for higher education, and to infuse fresh thinking and creative approaches to research.

  4. We can and should strive toward the possibility of joy and sustainability—with the caveat that I don't think you can talk about joy without acknowledging the systems of power that are currently in place, and working to bring about greater justice and equity in those systems.

It's important to me to connect these threads because too often I hear institutions talk about career preparation or public engagement in a way that is disconnected from the scholarly heart of graduate education, as well as from structural matters within the university; it's added as an afterthought or a corrective. Students rightfully feel betrayed by that; it feels hollow.

I also believe that equity is at the heart of all of these issues. When we talk about scholarly impact, public engagement, and career pathways, whether we acknowledge it or not we are also talking about who decides what is considered valuable, and whose voices are heard.

Why unexpected flourishing?

Thriving doesn't always look the way we expect.

  • Cycles of decay and regeneration
  • Interdependent, multispecies life
  • Mycoremediation
  • Beauty and nourishment, even in decay

As you might have guessed from the title, I'll be talking about mushrooms! This is where the idea of unexpected flourishing comes in, because thriving doesn't always look the way we expect it to look.

Let's start with a photo. (Most of the photos you'll see today are ones I've taken in the woods, mostly in and around New York. I am NOT a mycologist, so… apologies for any glaring scientific errors.)

One way to see this picture is as a rotting log. Another is to see it as a thriving ecosystem. And it gives a glimpse of what fascinates and inspires me about mushrooms.

  • Fungi have a critical role in the ecosystem, consuming dead matter and returning nutrients to the soil.

  • Some channel nutrients between plant species through what's called the mycorrhizal network. Mycorrhizal connections remind me that interdependence is a beautiful and fragile thing, something we can't control, and more than the sum of its parts.

  • There's growing research around mycoremediation, the process by which fungi digest and neutralize soil toxins, renewing fertile ground.

  • I also just love the beauty of these mushrooms, and how easy it is to overlook them. They have a quiet beauty—intricate colors and patterns and textures—but if you want to see them you have to walk slowly, look closely, stoop down, pick up fallen branches. This posture of attention is central to what I'm arguing today.

The rotting log

Universities in their current form are the substrate — the fallen log from which something new might grow.

  • Decades of public disinvestment and underfunding
  • Increasing reliance on contingent labor
  • Skyrocketing tuition and student debt
  • Persistence of structural inequities — sexism, racism, ableism, etc.
The decay is real. It may also serve as a condition for new growth.

In the metaphor I'll explore today, universities in their current form are the rotting log that provides a substrate for new growth. This sounds cynical, but I say this as someone who is deeply invested in higher education, especially public higher ed—I have a PhD from a public university, worked at CUNY for the better part of a decade, and now work with universities across the country to help them develop more sustainable, equitable programs.

The decay is, honestly, quite real. Decades of disinvestment have left universities deeply underfunded. Higher education erodes its own foundations through an increasing reliance on contingent faculty, whose inadequate working conditions become student learning conditions. Meanwhile, tuition and costs of living are skyrocketing, leaving many students deep in debt. And on top of all of this, the structural inequities that have always existed in the academy continue unabated.

But if universities are akin to rotting logs, might we find emerging life amongst the ruins?

Many of the most precious mushrooms cannot be cultivated; their partnerships with other species are too deep and specific. In The Mushroom at the End of the World, Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing traces the matsutake, which doesn't grow in pristine old-growth forest, but in what she calls "blasted landscapes"—areas of deforestation and disruption. In these cases, sometimes the best we can do is not plant a seed that we know will grow, but rather look for or create the conditions where the things we seek are most likely to thrive.

That's what I'm interested in thinking through for universities, and for the humanities in particular: finding ways to identify and create conditions for flourishing, even where it seems unlikely.

A moment of acute precarity

We are facing additional challenges in our current moment:

  • Federal defunding of awarded research grants
  • Direct threats to academic freedom and shared governance
  • Targeting of international students, trans students, and DEI-focused programs
  • Restructuring and closure of departments, programs, and entire institutions
This is not a normal period of institutional stress.

On top of the long decline universities face, this moment brings acute pressures. Nationally, we've seen the defunding of awarded federal grants—including the mass termination of NEH grants last year, which hit the humanities directly—along with threats to academic freedom, and the targeting of international students and DEI-focused programs. There are urgent human risks as well, particularly for students, faculty, and staff whose identities are under attack by this administration.

Our standard institutional structures and modes of operating will not suffice. I want to name this directly. Many folks are navigating real fear—about funding, programs, visa status, the viability of their research, and personal safety. These are really important issues, and I don't want to paper over them with optimism. But I do want to offer a framework for thinking about what's possible, even now.

Toward a mycelial university

What if we envisioned a different way of working?

  • Hyphae: liminal, small-scale, exploratory, but incredibly strong and resilient
  • Mycorrhizal networks: unexpected and deeply reciprocal partnerships
  • Fruiting bodies: ephemeral, surprising
  • Mycoremediation: digesting toxins, creating conditions for growth
Microscopic images of mycelium. Cartabia et al., 2021. https://doi.org/10.3390/jof7121008

What I propose in light of this is to let the hierarchical, prestige-oriented universities go ahead and decompose in favor of what I'm calling a mycelial university. While we might usually think of fungi in terms of the mushrooms we see above ground, most of the organism is actually underground, in the form of mycelium. Mycelium is made of delicate root-like threads, called hyphae, that twine through every inch of soil under our feet, digesting waste and forming interspecies nutrient channels. It's a vast interconnected network that is delicate and easily overlooked. But it's also strong enough to be used as a building material.

By mycelial university, I mean one that fosters tiny, hidden areas of growth, like the hyphae that are just one cell wide, growing belowground as they seek resources. In a university, this means making space for liminal work, small-scale work, work that may or may not bear fruit, but that connects people in authentic relationship. It also means fostering mycorrhizal connections—enabling unexpected partnerships both within the university and beyond it, deeply reciprocal knowledge exchanges across communities. It means finding strength even in precarity, through mutual support and resource sharing.

[NEW — revise] This is what I mean by a humanities ecosystem. The health of the humanities doesn't rest on any single department, center, or star scholar. It lives in the connective tissue: between departments and programs, between faculty, staff, and students, and between the university and the libraries, museums, schools, and community organizations where humanistic work also happens. Coalitions like these are how mycelium survives disruption, and they're how we will, too.

To get there, universities need to change their value structures. Rather than aiming for the greatest prestige—through grants, publications, faculty awards, and selectivity—what might happen if universities valued, and incentivized, access, collaboration, indeterminacy, collective thriving? What might this mean for public partnerships, and for advocacy?

For doctoral education in particular, a mycelial university would celebrate the creative, unexpected places where people apply their knowledge and training. More than that, it would make new pathways possible by fostering connections across people, disciplinary fields, and organizations.

ACLS Doctoral Futures

Recommendations ↗ Resources ↗
acls.org/doctoral-futures

Over the past year, I've been working with the American Council of Learned Societies (or ACLS), along with the AHA, MLA, and SBL, on an initiative called Doctoral Futures, with the goal of documenting promising practices and leveraging scholarly societies to push for wide-scale institutional change across the country. As part of this work, I've done landscape research and conducted twenty listening sessions with almost 100 current students, faculty, administrators, and recent PhDs.

The Doctoral Futures initiative is working to turn what we've heard in listening sessions into concrete recommendations and models for programs. More information is at acls.org/doctoral-futures.

What the research tells us

  • 1Advisor quality is decisive.
  • 2Funding is systematically inadequate.
  • 3Peer communities are lifelines.
  • 4Early career guidance matters.
  • 5Curricular clarity supports success.

Through this work, a few things have emerged as key elements for student success:

  1. Advisor quality. A supportive advisor is perhaps the most crucial element in student progress and well-being. However, advising practices vary significantly and are not often institutionally rewarded, leaving students in a precarious position without clear guidelines to navigate challenges.

  2. Material support. Adequate funding is essential to student progress, but the typical stipend falls short of a living wage. Students face food insecurity and impossible trade-offs. If you can't pay rent, care for your kid, or go to the doctor, there's no way you can do your best intellectual work.

  3. Non-competitive peer networks. Informal peer networks provide crucial support, especially when formal structures fail. Grad students regularly turn to their peers to understand the hidden curriculum, to form writing groups, and so much more. In many institutions right now, program size reductions are making this increasingly difficult.

  4. Career guidance. While grad career advising has improved in many institutions, for many students the discussion starts too late, toward the end of their studies. It should be integrated into the entire curriculum—not just a standalone career center, but in discussions within the department—from the very beginning of a student's program. Even before someone applies, they should have a sense of where alumni tend to be employed and what their own career prospects might be.

  5. Curricular clarity. Students—and really, faculty—should know why each step of their program matters. What is the goal of the exam? What constitutes a successful dissertation? Alongside this clarity, flexibility can provide essential breathing room, either for non-traditional research approaches or for the unexpected curveballs that are a part of life.

I'll come back to several of these on this afternoon's panel on graduate education.

What we heard about careers

Students and Recent PhDs

  • Not sure what jobs exist beyond the tenure track
  • Deep expertise but limited hands-on experience
  • Career conversations arrive "two years too late"
  • Exploration sometimes feels stigmatized

Faculty and Administrators

  • A desire to support students wherever they go
  • Not sure how to advise for unfamiliar pathways
  • Difficulty braiding scholarship with skills training
  • Backdrop of structural precarity and overwork
Career change is multi-year identity work, not a single decision point.

  • What we heard from students and what we heard from faculty are perspectives that mirror each other.

  • Students described an uncomfortable silence when they raised non-academic careers. One student said career preparation only came up in their sixth year—"maybe two years too late." Recent PhDs identified a gap between their deep expertise and the professional experience employers in other sectors look for.

  • Faculty, for their part, want to support students in a wider range of pathways, but feel a great deal of uncertainty when called upon to advise for careers they've never held. Most recognize that the advice they received no longer applies. They sometimes have a hard time braiding scholarly work with more applied approaches that help students build skills and relationships. And the backdrop of institutional precarity and ongoing labor casualization means faculty are trying to do this while overworked and anxious about the future.

  • One of the most striking themes was that career transition is identity work that takes years—one recent graduate said it took three years to separate who they are from what they do. A single workshop or panel can't carry that, so sustained engagement is key.

What works

Effective programs share a few elements.

  • Cohorts that build peer community
  • Mentors (plural) with varied perspectives
  • Structured experience that bridges the gap
  • Networks that last beyond the program
  • Skills translation: learning to name and own your strengths
How does this become the norm, not the exception?

Students and recent PhDs who have been part of programs like the Mellon Mays Fellowship or the ACLS Leading Edge Fellowship described their experiences as transformative. There are a handful of elements they have in common: a cohort, multiple mentors with different perspectives, professional experience, and a network that continues afterward. Institutional programs that work tend to share those features, plus help with translating skills.

But many strong initiatives come to an end when grant funding runs out, or when a key leader moves on. That's the structural question: how does this work become a core institutional commitment rather than an exception? That's part of what the ACLS Doctoral Futures program is working toward now.

Redefining success

Prestige-based model

  • Replication
  • Research primacy
  • Hidden curriculum
  • Structural inequity

Purpose-based model

  • Mentorship accountability
  • Holistic admissions
  • Public & collaborative work
  • Wellbeing & care
Cultural change cannot happen without structural change.

So who defines success in the academy, and in what ways? We know academia isn't a particularly high-paying industry; instead, the primary currency tends to be oriented around prestige.

Academic success tends to be conservative in its nature—it rewards things that replicate what's come before. We see this in admissions, in mentorship discussions, in dissertation standards and tenure criteria—all up and down the chain. Research takes precedence over teaching and mentorship, implicitly devaluing the relational parts of the scholarly journey. A lack of explicit guidelines means that the people who come in knowing more about the system have an easier time navigating it. And students and faculty from historically marginalized groups frequently face an inhospitable environment that can lead to burnout and attrition.

I believe there are ways to foster greater equity and more creative scholarship just by breaking down some of these tacit expectations. Offer evidence-based training for mentorship and advising, and hold advisors accountable. Rather than leaving students to guess what makes a high-quality dissertation, spell it out. Document the purpose of each step in the doctoral education process.

In addition to structural clarity, what if we thought much more broadly about why scholarly work matters, and to whom, and then recruited and structured our programs according to that broader vision? Then the curriculum might open up further, creating an organic emphasis on creativity, public engagement, and collaborative work done in relationship with others. We might even imagine a world in which wellbeing, care, and pleasure are understood as an intrinsic part of scholarly success.

From my own disciplinary background in the humanities, I think a lot about why my field matters. It's not about a performance of expertise. It matters because it's how we collectively find meaning and understanding in our lives. It's how we approach complicated, nuanced questions about the world we live in. It's how we express ourselves, how we connect with others, how we celebrate, and how we thrive.

Some of this is not that hard.

Certain steps are not as difficult or costly as some would have us believe:

  • Clear and purposeful program guidelines
  • Space for peer-to-peer support
  • Curiosity about students' goals and community responsibilities
How can we build structures grounded in purpose, not prestige?

My controversial take is that some of this work is simply not that hard.

A lot of what is needed right now is difficult—adequate funding, institutional investment, legislative change. But as universities work to prepare graduate students for a complex and changing world, certain steps are not as difficult or costly as some would have us believe. Making sure that program guidelines are clear and purposeful can be undertaken without broader institutional buy-in. Making space for peer-to-peer support can happen today. Being curious about a student's goals or community responsibilities costs nothing.

The more we work to unwind the structures of prestige, the closer we can get to a model of scholarly work that is grounded in purpose.

Start small

Photo by Giulia May on Unsplash.

Thinking about all of this at once can feel overwhelming. To think along these lines, I sometimes need to zoom WAY in and think small. Yes, we need broad-scale change. But rather than become frozen by how daunting that seems, sometimes it's worth zooming in to think about the countless small interactions where we CAN have an impact. Through my students, I have learned to value things I might otherwise brush off as small—because quiet acts of change, resistance, and support can have immeasurable impact. Our relationships and daily actions—what Emily Yoon Perez calls "little intimacies"—matter.

adrienne maree brown describes this in Emergent Strategy as working fractally: what we practice at the small scale sets the patterns for the whole system. The patterns we're working toward at the broad scale should also be present in the smallest ones—in how we run a meeting, advise a student, or partner with a community organization.

Moving toward joy

We get there by committing to action.

For yourself: What is one thing you can do right now to move closer to what sustains you?
For the ecosystem: What can you do to hold space for mycelial work, and to build structures that support it?

Taking cues from the abundance mindset of Black and Indigenous feminisms and feminisms of color—can we imagine a scholarly environment that is not grounded in competitiveness and scarcity, but is instead grounded in abundance and collective thriving?

Once again, I think the key to this is starting small. So I'll leave you with two questions. First: what is one thing you can do right now to move closer to integrity with your core values, or to what sustains you? This is not something to put off to a hypothetical less-stressful future. How can you find spaces for nourishment and support even within a context that often feels antithetical to replenishment?

And second: whatever your role—student, faculty, staff, administrator, community partner—how can you use it to make space for curiosity, mutual support, and exploration? What can you do to hold space for mycelial work, and to build the structures that let it last?

I have seen so many people step in to support one another when leadership and social structures fell short—what might it look like to design a system with those values at the core? This work requires understanding systems of power—knowing when to work within and against them. We can start today, by working in a way that embodies the values we hope to build into the academy, and by celebrating all the places where we already see those values in action.

We face so many difficult problems in this moment. Engaging with one another as full and complex humans does not need to be one of them—and it may be the key to our collective wellbeing.
