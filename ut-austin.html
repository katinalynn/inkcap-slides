What I'm drawing on

  • My provocation today is one of rotting logs and critical hope, of ruin and renewal

  • Draws on my first book, Putting the Humanities PhD to Work, and my forthcoming book, Unexpected Flourishing: Growth from Decay in the Mycelial University.

Why unexpected flourishing?

Thriving doesn't always look the way we expect.

  • Cycles of decay and regeneration
  • Interdependent, multispecies life
  • Mycoremediation
  • Beauty and nourishment, even in decay

Given the myriad challenges facing higher education today, how might we nevertheless create conditions for flourishing? I take inspiration from an unlikely source: mycelium, the tiny root-like threads of fungi.

Let's start with a photo. When you walk through the woods and see a fallen tree, you might see it as a loss. But look closer and you'll likely find an entire ecosystem, filled with fungi and plants and insects. (photos are my own)

  • Fungi consume dead matter and return nutrients to the soil.

  • Through mycoremediation, fungi digest and neutralize soil toxins, renewing fertile ground.

  • Some channel nutrients between plant species through mycorrhizal networks — an interdependence is at once fragile and strong; more than the sum of its parts.

  • And mushrooms have a quiet beauty that's easy to overlook. To see them you have to walk slowly, look closely, stoop down. That posture of attention is central to what I'm arguing.

The rotting log

Universities in their current form are the substrate — the fallen log from which something new might grow.

  • Decades of public disinvestment and underfunding
  • Increasing reliance on contingent labor
  • Skyrocketing tuition and student debt
  • Persistence of structural inequities — sexism, racism, ableism, etc.
The decay is real. It may also serve as a condition for new growth.

In this metaphor, universities are the rotting log. This sounds cynical, but I say this as someone deeply invested in public education — I have a PhD from a public university, worked at CUNY for the better part of a decade, and now work with universities across the country.

The decay is real. Decades of disinvestment have left universities deeply underfunded. Growing reliance on contingent faculty means their working conditions become student learning conditions. Tuition and costs of living keep rising, leaving many students deep in debt. And the structural inequities that have always existed in the academy continue.

But if universities are akin to rotting logs, might we find emerging life amongst the ruins?

In The Mushroom at the End of the World, Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing traces the matsutake — a valuable culinary mushroom that cannot be cultivated. It doesn't grow in pristine old-growth forest, but rather grows in what Tsing calls "blasted landscapes," areas of deforestation and disruption. The conditions don't look hospitable to new growth, but that is exactly where matsutake thrives.

That’s what I’m interested in for universities: creating the conditions where what we seek is most likely to thrive, and learning what can still flourish amidst the ruins.

A moment of acute precarity

We are facing additional challenges in our current moment:

  • Federal defunding of awarded research grants
  • State legislation reshaping curriculum and shared governance
  • Rapid restructuring of departments and programs
  • Direct threats to academic freedom
  • Precarity for international students, trans students, more
This is not a normal period of institutional stress.

On top of the long decline universities face, this moment brings acute pressures. Nationally, we've seen the defunding of awarded federal grants, direct threats to academic freedom, and the targeting of international students and DEI-focused programs.

SB 37 in Texas has required governing boards to review the undergraduate core and has reshaped faculty governance, and UT Austin is the latest institution to face restructuring of the core curriculum and of departments in the College of Liberal Arts. I know people in this room are feeling that directly.

There are urgent human risks as well, particularly for students, faculty, and staff whose identities are under attack by this administration.

Many are navigating real fear — about funding, programs, the viability of research, and personal safety.

Still, I want to offer a framework for thinking about what's possible, even now.

Toward a mycelial university

What if we envisioned a different way of working?

  • Hyphae: liminal, small-scale, exploratory, but incredibly strong and resilient
  • Mycorrhizal networks: unexpected and deeply reciprocal partnerships
  • Fruiting bodies: ephemeral, surprising
  • Mycoremediation: digesting toxins, creating conditions for growth
Microscopic images of mycelium. Cartabia et al., 2021. https://doi.org/10.3390/jof7121008

Mycelium is the key to the rotting log's transformation, slowly breaking down decaying matter into fertile soil and channeling nutrients between species to support new life. Most of a fungus lives underground as hyphae — threads just one cell wide, twining through the soil. Delicate and easily overlooked, yet strong enough to be used as a building material.

Much like the multispecies connections fostered by mycelium, resilience in this moment requires deep interdependence and relationship. In what I call a mycelial university, that means:

  • Making space for liminal, small-scale work that may or may not bear fruit, but that connects people in authentic relationship.

  • Fostering unexpected, deeply reciprocal partnerships, within the university and beyond it.

  • Finding strength even in precarity, through mutual support and resource sharing.

For doctoral education, a mycelial university would celebrate the creative, unexpected places where people apply their training — and make new pathways possible by connecting people across fields and organizations.

What the research tells us

  • 1Advisor quality is decisive.
  • 2Funding is systematically inadequate.
  • 3Peer communities are lifelines.
  • 4Early career guidance matters.
  • 5Curricular clarity supports success.

Over the past year, I've been working with the American Council of Learned Societies, along with the AHA, MLA, and SBL, on an initiative called Doctoral Futures. As part of this work, I've done landscape research and conducted twenty listening sessions with almost 100 current students, faculty, administrators, and recent PhDs. A few things emerged as key elements for student success:

  1. Advisor quality is perhaps the most crucial element in student progress and well-being — yet advising practices vary widely and are rarely rewarded institutionally.

  2. Material support. The typical stipend falls short of a living wage. If you can't pay rent or go to the doctor, you can't do your best intellectual work.

  3. Non-competitive peer networks are lifelines, especially when formal structures fail — and shrinking program sizes are making them harder to sustain.

  4. Career guidance too often starts at the end. It belongs in the department from the very beginning, even before admission.

  5. Curricular clarity. Students — and faculty — should know why each step of the program matters, with flexibility for creative work and life's curveballs.

The structural question is how does this work become a core institutional commitment rather than an exception? That's part of what the ACLS Doctoral Futures program is working toward now.

Some of this is not that hard.

Certain steps are not as difficult or costly as some would have us believe:

  • Clear and purposeful program guidelines
  • Space for peer-to-peer support
  • Curiosity about students' goals and community responsibilities
How can we build structures grounded in purpose, not prestige?

My controversial take is that some of this work is simply not that hard.

A lot of what is needed right now is difficult — adequate funding, institutional investment, legislative change. But as universities work to prepare graduate students for a complex and changing world, certain steps are not as difficult or costly as some would have us believe. Making sure that program guidelines are clear and purposeful can be undertaken without broader institutional buy-in. Making space for peer-to-peer support can happen today. Being curious about a student's goals or community responsibilities costs nothing.

Thinking expansively about meaningful scholarly work means asking who defines success. Academia's primary currency is prestige, and prestige tends to be conservative: it rewards replication, prioritizes research over teaching and mentorship, leaves the rules implicit so that those who already know the system navigate it best, and too often pushes out people from historically marginalized groups. The more we work to unwind these structures, the closer we can get to a model of scholarly work that is grounded in purpose, not prestige.

Start small

Photo by Giulia May on Unsplash.

This often starts small. adrienne maree brown describes working fractally — what we practice at small scale, we build at large scale. I have seen so many people step in to support one another when leadership and structures fell short. What might it look like to design a system with those values at its core? We can start today, by working in a way that embodies the values we hope to build into the academy, and by celebrating the places where we already see them in action.

Taking cues from the abundance mindset of Black and Indigenous feminisms and feminisms of color: can we imagine a scholarly environment grounded not in competition and scarcity, but in abundance and collective thriving? In the humanities especially, our work matters not as a performance of expertise, but because it's how we collectively find meaning, approach complicated questions about the world, express ourselves, and connect with one another.

We face so many difficult problems in this moment. Engaging with one another as full and complex humans does not need to be one of them — and it may be the key to our collective wellbeing.
