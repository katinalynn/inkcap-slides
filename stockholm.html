Unexpected Flourishing:
Growth from Decay in the Mycelial University

On rotting logs, higher education, and critical hope
Katina L. Rogers  ·  Inkcap Consulting

Why unexpected flourishing?

Thriving doesn't always look the way we expect.

  • Cycles of decay and regeneration
  • Interdependent, multispecies life
  • Mycoremediation
  • Beauty, even in decay
  • For me: critical hope

The rotting log

Universities in their current form are the substrate — the fallen log from which something new might grow.

  • Decades of public disinvestment and underfunding
  • Increasing reliance on contingent labor
  • Skyrocketing tuition and student debt
  • Persistence of structural inequities
The decay is real. It may also serve as a condition for new growth.

A moment of acute precarity

We are facing additional challenges in our current moment:

  • Federal defunding of awarded research grants
  • Direct threats to academic freedom and shared governance
  • Targeting of international students and DEI-focused programs
  • Dismantling of programs and entire institutions
This is not a normal period of institutional stress. We need to think differently.
Woodland restoration — the blasted landscape, and the beginning of something new.

Blasted landscapes

In The Mushroom at the End of the World, Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing offers an ethnography of the matsutake mushrooms and the blasted landscapes in which they thrive.

  • Cannot be cultivated
  • Complex multispecies dependencies
  • Disturbance is required
Where are the pockets of possibility in higher ed? What connections bring an unexpected flush of new growth?
Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing, The Mushroom at the End of the World: On the Possibility of Life in Capitalist Ruins (Princeton University Press, 2015)
Tsing follows the lifecycle and economies of the matsutake mushroom. What I take from this is a kind of critical hope. The log has already fallen — but maybe those inherited structures can be digested and neutralized, enabling something new to grow in their midst.

Toward a mycelial university

What if we envisioned a different way of working?

  • Hyphae: liminal, small-scale, exploratory, but incredibly strong and resilient
  • Mycorrhizal networks: unexpected and deeply reciprocal partnerships
  • Fruiting bodies: ephemeral, surprising
  • Mycoremediation: digesting toxins, creating conditions for growth
Microscopic images of mycelium. Cartabia et al., 2021. https://doi.org/10.3390/jof7121008
The metaphor I explore throughout the book. The inherited structures of the university are the rotting log. The mycelium is the hidden, persistent work of connection and care. Sometimes, when conditions are right, a mushroom emerges — nourishment, beauty, possibility.

My anchor points

Moving from individual action to structural change.

  • Talking about career diversity is not enough
  • Structures reveal and reinforce values
  • Public engagement is good for students, the academy, and society
  • Joy and sustainability are possible
This is an equity question.
Who decides what is considered valuable? Whose voices are heard?
I've been working on graduate education reform for almost fifteen years, and the throughline looks so much clearer in retrospect than it did when I was moving through it. I was always just making the best decision I could within the context and constraints of the moment.

Redefining success

Prestige-based model

  • Replication
  • Research primacy
  • Hidden curriculum
  • Structural inequity

Purpose-based model

  • Mentorship accountability
  • Holistic admissions
  • Public & collaborative work
  • Wellbeing & care
Cultural change cannot happen without structural change.
Higher ed is oriented around prestige, and that makes it very hard to make space for other value systems. This is where the hidden curriculum comes into play — some people come in knowing more about the system than others. There are ways to foster greater equity and more creative scholarship just by breaking down some of these tacit expectations.

Moving toward joy

Can we imagine a scholarly environment not grounded in competitiveness and scarcity?

  • Sometimes this requires thinking small to identify where we can have an impact
  • Outsized importance of quiet acts of change & resistance
  • Fractal approach (adrienne maree brown)— the patterns toward which we strive are present in the smallest details
My hope is that we can collectively move toward structures that enable joy.
Through my students, I learned to value things I might otherwise brush off as small — the quiet acts of change and support can have immeasurable impact. These are modest steps toward a framing that emphasizes abundance and thriving.
